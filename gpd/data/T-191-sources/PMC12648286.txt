Published by American Chemical Society This article is licensed under CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 Abstract Structural DNA nanotechnology enables the programmable construction of nano- to microscale assemblies with high resolution, yet predicting their mechanical behavior prior to synthesis remains challenging. In this paper, we introduce SNUPI, a computational framework that predicts the shape and mechanical responses of structured DNA assemblies using finite element-based analysis. SNUPI uses design files from caDNAno and performs static analysis, normal-mode analysis, and Langevin dynamics simulations to evaluate equilibrium configurations, dominant deformation modes, thermal fluctuations, and intercalator-induced effects. We demonstrate that SNUPI can offer an efficient and accessible platform for pre- and postsynthetic evaluation, facilitating rational design and analysis of complex DNA architectures. Introduction Structural DNA nanotechnology has rapidly advanced over the past few decades, enabling the precise design and assembly of nanoscale architectures by harnessing the sequence-specific base-pairing properties of DNA. , Owing to its programmability, high structural stability, and biocompatibility, DNA has emerged as a promising nanomaterial for constructing a wide variety of two- and three-dimensional nanostructures. − Representative strategies to synthesize and design structured DNA assemblies were developed, such as DNA origami, which folds a long scaffold strand into arbitrary shapes using short staple strands, tile-based assemblies formed by periodic motif units, and wireframe architectures based on rigid polyhedral geometries. DNA structures have been widely applied in diverse fields, including plasmonic sensing, , targeted drug delivery, − molecular devices, − and DNA-based computational logic circuits. , Despite these advances, experimental synthesis and mechanical characterization of structured DNA assemblies remain time-consuming and costly. It is particularly challenging to predict the stability or equilibrium geometry of designed structures during the early stages of development. Moreover, optimizing structural properties such as length, twist, and bending rigidity prior to synthesis remains difficult to achieve quantitatively through experiments alone. To address these challenges, computational tools have been actively developed to simulate and predict the dynamic behavior of DNA assemblies. Notable platforms include oxDNA, a coarse-grained molecular dynamics (MD) model, mrDNA which incorporates a hierarchical multiscale framework, and CanDo and SNUPI, finite element–based approaches that integrate structural information for mechanical analysis. In this study, we focus on the SNUPI (Structured NUcleic-acids Programming Interface) protocol for the static and dynamic analysis of structured DNA assemblies. We demonstrate that SNUPI enables accurate and efficient prediction of the mechanical behavior and deformation potential of designed DNA architectures before experimental implementation. Based on caDNAno design files, SNUPI constructs the initial structural configuration and performs static analysis, normal-mode analysis (NMA), and dynamic analysis to resolve global motions of the structure. Furthermore, we show that the mechanical effects of ethidium bromide (EtBr) intercalation, including helical unwinding and rigidity reduction, can be quantitatively evaluated through simulation. ,− Results Overall Flow SNUPI provides an integrated and accessible computational framework for analyzing the mechanical behavior of structured DNA assemblies. Its streamlined workflow from design input to postprocessing allows users to efficiently evaluate and optimize DNA nanostructures without requiring advanced programming expertise. The platform supports all major operating systems (Windows, macOS, and Linux) and offers configurable options suitable for both entry-level and expert users. Specifically, SNUPI offers structural prediction and design-oriented analytics for complex 2D and 3D architectures. NMA yields low-order modes and fluctuation maps that reveal structural warping and soft spots. Correlation maps can also identify mechanically coupled modules versus decoupled, flexible sites. In addition, the chemo-mechanical analysis enables rapid studies of binding effects allowing users to tune shape and rigidity. SNUPI provides a versatile computational framework for analyzing the mechanical behavior of structured DNA assemblies ( Figure a). Static analysis enables rapid prediction of the equilibrium shape, which can be followed by NMA to extract low-order vibrational modes and estimate global stiffness. Furthermore, dynamic simulations based on Langevin dynamics allow direct observation of time-resolved structural fluctuations, from which shape descriptors such as root-mean-square deviation (RMSD), root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF), and Pearson and generalized correlation coefficients can be quantitatively evaluated. Integrating these static and dynamic analyses, SNUPI also supports chemo-mechanical simulations, enabling the prediction of structural responses induced by molecular intercalation, such as EtBr-binding. 1 Overall procedure. ( a ) SNUPI framework. SNUPI integrates static, dynamic, and chemo-mechanical analyses to characterize the shape and mechanical properties of structured DNA assemblies. Static analysis determines the equilibrium configuration, followed by NMA to extract global stiffness and mode shapes. Langevin dynamic simulations provide time-resolved trajectories for computing RMSD, RMSF, and correlation coefficients. The framework further supports chemo-mechanical modeling by incorporating EtBr-binding effects. ( b ) SNUPI workflow. SNUPI analyzes structured DNA assemblies using a design file (json), sequence file (csv) and lattice type (Honeycomb or Square) extracted from caDNAno. User-defined settings can be customized via an option file (snp). To run SNUPI ( Figure b), we begin by designing a DNA assembly using caDNAno and obtain its topology (json file) and sequence (csv file). These design files, along with the lattice type (H: Honeycomb or S: Square), should be specified in the input file (Input.txt). SNUPI runs on separately compiled versions for different operating systems (Windows, macOS, and Linux) (). An option file (snp file) can be added to customize the user-defined settings, or SNUPI will run with the default settings (default.snp). In the user-defined options file, we can customize the specific methods in the analysis and files to be exported for the postprocessing or further study (). By default, SNUPI consists of three stages: initiation, analysis, and postprocessing. In the initiation stage, the topology, sequence information, and user-defined options are read from the caDNAno files to generate the initial configuration of the designed DNA assembly. In the analysis stage, all structural and electrostatic connections between base-pairs are converted into finite elements, and physical properties obtained from molecular dynamics simulations are assigned to them. The equilibrium configuration is then predicted by minimizing the mechanical and electrostatic energy through an iterative nonlinear procedure. Thirty incremental steps are used as the default value, but if convergence is difficult to achieve, the incremental step size is adaptively adjusted to gradually reach equilibrium. Once analysis is complete, SNUPI saves the predicted equilibrium configuration of the DNA assembly. By customizing the settings, the equilibrium configuration can be converted into atomic coordinates (pdb file), suitable for performing MD simulations. Postprocessing or additional analysis can then be performed by modifying the option file, including NMA to extract principal mode shapes, Langevin dynamics simulations, or chemo-mechanical analysis. The current version of SNUPI is configured to analyze a single DNA structure per execution. However, users may run multiple analyses in parallel, thereby enabling efficient and simultaneous evaluation of numerous DNA designs during iterative workflows. Equilibrium Configuration Analysis The DNA assembly primarily consists of double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) and single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) that form the structural framework, and various connecting components (structural motifs such as crossovers and nicks) that locally induce mechanical deformations. Each component contributes to global geometry and determines the mechanical behavior of DNA assembly. To quantitatively analyze these properties, each base-pair is modeled as a rigid block with six degrees of freedom (three translational and three rotational) and the finite element method is employed to represent the connection between two base-pairs and incorporate sequence-dependent characteristics extracted from MD simulations. This approach allows accurate and efficient prediction of the three-dimensional conformation, equilibrium dynamics, and mechanical characteristics of the DNA assembly. To perform finite element analysis on a given DNA assembly, SNUPI constructs the initial configuration (base-pair positions and connectivity) and assigns mechanical properties to each base-pair connection exported from caDNAno design. In cases where the initial configuration significantly affects the nonlinear analysis such as closed-loop or wireframe designs, SNUPI employs a partition and relocation procedure to refine the initial configuration. Since caDNAno designs represent a two-dimensional blueprint, this procedure can effectively generate an initial three-dimensional configuration that reflects the intended design physically. To illustrate, we examined an S-shaped DNA origami structure as an example design with all default options ( Figure ). 2 Shape prediction of a structured DNA assembly. The S-shaped DNA origami structure was analyzed using default SNUPI settings. Finite element models incorporating both structural and electrostatic interactions were constructed based on base-pair connectivity, and the equilibrium configuration was predicted through 30 incremental steps from the initial configuration. The magnified view of the boxed region shows individual elements and connections. With the well-defined configuration and sequence-dependent properties, SNUPI conducts a nonlinear static analysis to determine the equilibrium configuration of the entire DNA assembly. Due to the nonlinear nature of DNA mechanics and the challenge of directly solving for the final configuration, SNUPI adopts an incremental approach, gradually applying mechanical properties over multiple steps. By default, 30 incremental steps are used, but if convergence is not achieved in each step, the step size is adaptively subdivided to reach equilibrium to meet convergence criteria. To check whether the solution has converged, default tolerance thresholds are set to 10 –3 for force and 10 –6 for energy, with a maximum of 15 iterations per incremental step. For the S-shaped structure converging configurations are analyzed during 30 incremental steps. Once the nonlinear analysis converges, SNUPI performs postprocessing to generate the final configuration and output files for downstream analysis. These include coordinate information (pdb file) for molecular visualization using tools such as VMD, and a model trajectory file that records structural deformation throughout the incremental steps. In addition, a restart file is generated, enabling further simulations to begin from the final configuration under new options. The configuration output from SNUPI can also be used for further analysis using deep learning model or oxDNA. Normal Mode Analysis Normal mode analysis (NMA) has long been employed in classical mechanics and structural engineering to study the vibrational behavior of macroscopic systems. It has also been applied to molecular and nanoscale systems, including the analysis of dynamic modes in proteins. − Inspired by these diverse applications, we applied NMA to predict the global deformation modes, mechanical rigidity, and dynamic behavior of structured DNA assemblies in this study. Compared to principal component analysis (PCA), which requires time-resolved trajectories such as MD simulations, NMA is computationally more efficient and thus particularly useful during the early design phase to predict the mechanical response of complex DNA assemblies. The resulting eigenvalues correspond to the squared natural frequencies of vibration, and the eigenvectors represent the associated mode shapes. The lowest eigenvalue corresponds to the dominant global deformation mode. In three-dimensional systems, the first six modes typically represent rigid body motions (translations and rotations), and meaningful deformation modes begin from the seventh mode. For example, in bundle structures, the seventh mode could correspond to bending deformation, while one of the higher-order modes may represent torsional motions. Since structural deformation is usually dominated by the lowest-frequency modes, SNUPI is set to compute the 15 lowest normal modes by default. Each eigenvalue and its associated eigenvector together describe the frequency and spatial pattern of a specific deformation mode. For example, we performed NMA for the 16-helix bundle structure on the square lattice ( Figure a). 3 Normal mode analysis of a bundle structure. ( a ) Mode shapes of the 16-helix bundle structure. The seventh to 15th lowest normal modes excluding the first six rigid-body modes, were obtained. Due to the slender geometry of the structure, bending modes appeared predominantly, followed by torsional modes. ( b ) RMSF analysis. RMSF values were computed for each base-pair at equilibrium using the eigenvalues of the 200 lowest normal modes. Terminal and flexible regions exhibited higher fluctuations than the core regions. ( c ) Correlation analysis. Pearson and generalized correlation coefficients were computed to quantify the cooperative motion between base-pairs. High correlations were observed for structurally connected base-pairs, while distant or loosely coupled pairs showed weaker coupling. The NMA results can be used to estimate the global mechanical rigidity of DNA structures. , For linear bundle structures, the commonly used concept of persistence length can be applied, which is proportional to structural stiffness. Specifically, if the bundle is modeled as a single beam, the bending persistence length ( L PB ) can be predicted from the Euler–Bernoulli beam equation, following the relation L P B = E I k B T = M λ B L C 3 k B T ( β L C ) 4 where EI is the global bending rigidity, k B is the Boltzmann constant, T is the ambient temperature (300 K), M is the total mass, and λ B is the eigenvalue associated with the first bending mode, defined as the square of the natural frequency (ω 2 = λ). L C denotes the contour length of the structure, and βL C is a numerical constant determined as 4.733 for the first bending mode. Similarly, the torsional persistence length ( L PC ) was estimated by modeling the structure as a continuum medium, following the relation L P C = G J k B T = M λ T L C ( J o / A ) k B T π 2 where GJ is the global torsional rigidity, λ T is the eigenvalue associated with the first torsional mode. The term J o / A is a geometric factor determined by the cross-sectional shape and is proportional to the square of the effective radius of the helices. The effective radius was measured to be 1.1 nm based on the interhelical spacing of the structures. For the 16-helix bundle structure, estimated bending and torsional persistence lengths were 13,063 nm and 2,463 nm, respectively (). NMA also enables calculation of the RMSF, which quantifies the thermal vibrational amplitude of each base-pair in the equilibrium state. SNUPI computes RMSF using the eigenvalues of lowest 200 normal modes by default ( Figure b). Flexible regions such as terminal ends or loosely connected junctions typically exhibit higher RMSF values, while more rigid domains show smaller fluctuations. In addition, NMA provides correlation coefficients that quantify cooperative motions between base-pairs. These include Pearson and generalized correlations. Pearson correlation evaluates the directional similarity of displacements, ranging from −1 (perfectly opposite motion) to +1 (perfectly aligned motion). Additionally, a value of 0 indicates uncorrelated motion. This metric enables the identification of mechanically correlated regions, such as deformable junctions or hinge-like domains, that may function as modular structural units. For instance, base-pairs connected through crossovers exhibit high positive correlations, indicating coordinated motion, while distant or weakly connected pairs display weak or negative correlations, reflecting independent or opposing fluctuations ( Figure c). Such correlation patterns provide valuable insights for structural optimization: regions with strong correlation may serve as mechanically coupled modules, while low or negative correlation suggests flexible or decoupled domains. In addition to the Pearson correlation coefficient, we employed the generalized correlation coefficient to evaluate the statistical dependence between base-pair motions based on mutual information. While the Pearson correlation captures linear, directionally coherent displacements, the generalized correlation can identify nonlinear and nondirectional coupling between base-pair fluctuations. The coefficient ranges from 0 (statistically independent) to 1 (fully correlated motion). For instance, Pearson correlation highlights directly aligned or opposing motions between adjacent base-pairs, whereas generalized correlation reveals cooperative dynamics among nonadjacent or structurally distant regions that may not exhibit explicit geometric linkage ( Figure c). This metric is particularly effective in identifying functionally correlated domains that undergo coordinated deformation despite the absence of direct structural connectivity. In particular, pairs of regions exhibiting low Pearson correlation but high generalized correlation can be interpreted as dynamically coupled through nonlinear or indirect interactions. Therefore, SNUPI enables a detailed assessment of dynamic coupling within the DNA assembly. Dynamic Analysis Dynamic analysis can be employed to simulate the motion of DNA assemblies under solvent conditions. While NMA effectively captures global motions by evaluating the lowest-frequency modes, it cannot account for external forces. In contrast, dynamic analysis leverages Langevin dynamics to simulate time-dependent trajectories of the DNA assembly, incorporating both thermal fluctuations and internal mechanical forces, enabling prediction of how the assembly evolves over time in a solvent environment. In the structural model, each base-pair in the DNA assembly was modeled as a rigid block, and these blocks are connected via finite elements, providing the internal force vector of the assembly. To solve the Langevin dynamics equation, the mass matrix is assembled based on the molecular mass of elementary sequences. Solvent-mediated viscous drag is also modeled into a friction matrix derived from the generalized Rotne-Prager-Yamakawa mobility tensor, contributing to the random force vector by thermal fluctuations ( Figure a). The equations of motion are integrated over time to generate the time trajectory, enabling molecular-scale predictions driven by thermal noise. 4 Dynamic analysis of structured DNA assemblies. ( a ) Dynamics simulation modeling. The motion of the DNA assembly was simulated using Langevin dynamics based on a finite element framework. Solvent-induced viscous drag was modeled, and thermal noise was introduced as a random force. ( b ) RMSD convergence of the triangular DNA structure. A 100 ns dynamic simulation was performed starting from the equilibrium shape predicted by static analysis. The RMSD rapidly converged, indicating the system reached equilibrium. ( c ) Comparison of RMSF profiles. RMSF values computed from dynamic simulations were compared with those obtained from NMA, showing similar fluctuation trends across the structure. ( d ) Correlation analysis from dynamic trajectories. Pearson and generalized correlation coefficients were computed from dynamic simulations, which can capture nonlinear effects across the DNA assembly. As a dynamic simulation is finished, the RMSD can be computed during the simulation to assess the convergence of the dynamic trajectory. If the RMSD reaches a stable plateau over time, it indicates that the DNA assembly has reached equilibrium, validating the reliability of subsequent RMSF and correlation analyses. As with NMA, dynamic analysis allows the calculation of RMSF and correlation coefficients, with the advantage of capturing realistic dynamic responses driven by thermal fluctuations and structural mechanics. To illustrate, the dynamic simulation of a triangular DNA structure for 100 ns was finished in 8 min of real time using a personal computer to check convergence of RMSD ( Figure b). In particular, starting the dynamic simulation from the final shape obtained by static prediction rather than from the initial configuration generated from the caDNAno file can rapidly converge the RMSD. It notes that an option on GPU acceleration can be adopted to produce a trajectory fast for full-sized DNA origami structures. While RMSF values obtained from dynamic analysis could be similar to those derived from NMA ( Figure c), the underlying computational frameworks differ fundamentally. NMA computes RMSF based on vibrational modes at thermal equilibrium, capturing only stiffness-dominated, harmonic behavior. In contrast, dynamic analysis solves the Langevin equation in the time domain, accounting for thermal noise, viscous damping, and time-dependent dynamics, thus yielding a more realistic description of structural dynamics. Moreover, whereas NMA typically captures motion using the lowest modes, thereby focusing on low-frequency behavior, dynamic analysis is based on time trajectories, effectively incorporating motion across the entire frequency spectrum, including high-frequency vibrations and fine-scale structural responses. Correlation coefficients obtained from NMA are inherently limited to the resolved eigenmodes and primarily capture low-frequency dynamics. In contrast, dynamic analysis tracks the time-resolved positions and orientations of base-pairs, enabling a more comprehensive quantification of coordinated motions across the assembly ( Figure d). Notably, Pearson correlation from dynamic analysis can reveal long-range correlations beyond the resolution of NMA, while generalized correlation based on mutual information can detect nonlinear dynamic coupling between base-pairs that NMA cannot. Nonetheless, the primary difficulty of dynamic analysis lies in its computational intensity. Chemo-mechanical Analysis EtBr is a well-known intercalating agent that interacts strongly with the DNA double helix, inducing local changes in both geometry and mechanical properties. EtBr intercalates between adjacent base-pairs, locally increasing rise and thereby globally elongating the DNA assembly. This intercalation also reduces helical twist, resulting in a left-handed torsional deformation of the assembly. EtBr intercalation between two base-pairs was modeled EtBr elements, implemented by incorporating those molecular-level changes into the local stiffness matrix of the finite element. These local effects are reflected in the stiffness matrices of the finite elements of EtBr-bound base-pairs and are subsequently assembled into the global stiffness matrix of the DNA assembly to predict its final shape and mechanical behavior. The extent of EtBr-induced deformation is governed by the number of intercalated EtBr molecules, which can be quantitatively determined from experimental concentrations using a binding model. Based on this model, the number of bound EtBr molecules can be calculated for a given experimental concentration, allowing comparison between simulation and experiment. For instance, for the previously reported clamped-shape structure we also showed that EtBr-induced local effects led to a reduction in the end-to-end length of the structure ( Figure ). The number of bound EtBr molecules was estimated to be approximately 650 at an experimental concentration of 8 mM (). 5 Prediction of EtBr-induced deformation. A clamped DNA origami structure was analyzed to evaluate the effect of EtBr intercalation. The estimated number of intercalated EtBr molecules was approximately 650 at the experimental EtBr concentration of 8 mM. The simulation incorporating EtBr-induced local changes, modeled via EtBr elements, predicted a reduced end-to-end length of the structure, showing agreement with the experimental observation. Conclusion In this paper, we presented the SNUPI protocol for predicting the shape and mechanical behavior of structured DNA assemblies. By integrating static analysis, normal-mode analysis, and Langevin dynamics–based simulations, SNUPI enables comprehensive evaluation of equilibrium configurations, dominant deformation modes, and thermally induced fluctuations. Moreover, the protocol accounts for structural effects of EtBr intercalation by incorporating local geometric and stiffness alterations at the base-pair step level. Importantly, this chemo-mechanical modeling strategy is not limited to EtBr. Because SNUPI supports spatially defined stiffness and structural parameters as inputs, it is also applicable to other binding agents such as poly lysine (PLL) and polyethylene glycol (PEG), which are widely used to enhance structural stability or modulate the properties of DNA assemblies. , By incorporating the mechanical effects of such binders at specific locations within the structure, SNUPI enables analysis of how various binding interactions influence DNA architectures. This generalizability opens opportunities to explore chemo-mechanical design principles in DNA nanotechnology. SNUPI operates directly on structural design files, providing an intuitive and efficient framework for both pre- and postsynthetic evaluation of structured DNA assemblies. While SNUPI, parametrized from all-atom molecular dynamics simulations, effectively captures the global deformation behavior of DNA assemblies, it inherits certain limitations from the underlying model. The approach assumes that base-pair interactions can be represented as pairwise effective potentials between adjacent nodes, which may not fully describe multibody effects involving three or more base-pairs or highly localized deformations. The present model is thus accurate for DNA origami or larger-scale assemblies, whereas extensions to include nonlocal properties could improve resolution. Nevertheless, for DNA assemblies composed of many double helices, the present method provides a favorable balance between accuracy and speed, allowing rapid prediction of structural behavior, reduction of repetitive experimental trials, and efficient screening of multiple design variants within iterative workflows.
