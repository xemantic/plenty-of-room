This is true in particular in the presence of surface heterogeneity and specific adsorption. Using a mean-field model, we discuss how these factors shape capacitance curves, affect the potential of zero charge (PZC), and alter the Parsons–Zobel (PZ) plots. The model reveals four distinct regimes of interfacial capacitance behaviour, determined by the facet size and ratio, the Debye length, and differences in PZC and Helmholtz capacitance between facets. These regimes define clear conditions under which capacitance curves exhibit either one or two minima, with the single minimum either coinciding with or deviating from the global PZC. We further demonstrate how adsorption on low-coordinated facets modifies the symmetry and magnitude of capacitance profiles and suppresses PZ slopes. e01d47224afac6404de0a3d6d8680b1b07d55c03b509d0e7589c2b46ed5597d8 Introduction Understanding the electrical double layer (EDL) at metal–electrolyte interfaces is fundamental for interpreting and predicting the activity of a wide range of electrocatalytic reactions, including the hydrogen evolution and oxidation reactions (HER/HOR), − oxygen evolution/reduction reaction (OER/ORR), − and CO 2 reduction reaction (CO 2 RR). − While fundamental studies often focus on well-defined single-crystal electrodes, practical catalytic electrodes are typically polycrystalline, exposing a range of surface facets. − Moreover, even initially flat single-crystal electrodes can develop significant roughness under reaction conditions. − This also leads to the development of low-coordinated sites and facets, such as steps, kinks, and edges, which are particularly prone to be covered by adsorbates under electrochemical conditions. , Such adsorption events can profoundly modify the local EDL structure and affect macroscopic observables. − Consequently, elucidating the structure and behaviour of the EDL at electrodes with multiple (possibly adsorbate-covered) facets is essential for a correct interpretation of observables and a realistic understanding of electrochemical interfaces. One widely used approach to probing the EDL is the measurement of double-layer capacitance ( C dl ) curves. , Early comparisons between poly- and single-crystalline Ag by Valette and Hamelin employed a weighted average model. The calculated capacitance curve roughly matches the experimental data, with the capacitance minimum aligning more closely with that of the facet having the most negative potential of zero charge (PZC). Bagotskaya et al. and Vorotyntsev further proposed, in addition to the independent diffuse layer model (which is equivalent to the weighted average model), a shared diffuse layers model to describe the total capacitance of polycrystalline electrode in terms of the capacitance of individual single-crystal facets. The independent diffuse layers model consists of two facets connected in parallel, each comprising a Helmholtz capacitance and a diffuse-layer capacitance in series; the shared diffuse layers model consists of two Helmholtz capacitances in parallel, connected in series with a shared diffuse-layer capacitance. Bagotskaya et al. argued that the applicability of these models depends on the PZC difference between facets and the relevant system length scales. Similar limiting cases were more recently identified by Müller et al. who simulated C dl curves for polycrystalline electrodes using a continuum model that accounts for finite ion size and partial solvation effects on the metal surface. , However, rather than defining the limiting cases by comparing the facet size to a characteristic EDL length scale (e.g., Debye length λ D ), as done by Vorotyntsev, and Daikhin et al., − they defined a length scale of 1 nm, to determine whether the weighted average model can be used to approximate the capacitance curves. We will get back to these characteristic length scales and the two limiting cases later in this article. A central prediction of the classical Gouy–Chapman–Stern (GCS) model is that the potential of the capacitance minimum ( E dl,min ) coincides with the PZC of the interface, provided that specific adsorption is absent and the electrolyte is sufficiently dilute. This prediction has been validated for various single-crystal electrodes. − For polycrystalline electrodes, however, the situation becomes more complex, as the overall capacitance is effectively equivalent to several capacitances connected in parallel, as discussed above. In the continuum model of Müller et al., when facet lengths are much smaller than 1 nm (approaching zero), the PZC of the polycrystalline surface reduces to the weighted average of the corresponding single-crystal PZCs. Ibach et al. simulated the interfacial capacitance of nano-stripe electrodes and found that a single capacitance minimum appears when the width of stripes with different facets exceeds 3λ D with a PZC difference of 0.2 V between stripes. In this case, however, E dl,min was found to deviate from the global PZC by several tens of meV. More recently, Song et al. measured the pH-dependent E dl,min for polycrystalline Pt and Au electrodes in acidic solutions and compared it with the trend of potential of maximum entropy (PME), which is believed to be closely related to the PZC. , Both the E dl,min and PME shifted positively with pH on the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale for both electrodes. However, the measured capacitance and PME of polycrystalline Pt are inevitably influenced by surface adsorbates, , , which complicates direct comparison with the PZC of the interface. Building on these prior efforts, in this work we developed a continuum model to systematically investigate how surface heterogeneity and adsorbates influence the EDL of polycrystalline electrodes. Compared with previous studies, , our objectives are two-fold. First, we conduct a comprehensive parametric study of C dl curves as a function of (i) Size of the repeating facets L (1–100 nm), (ii) Debye length λ D (3–30 nm, corresponding to bulk solution concentration of 0.1–10 mM), (iii) PZC difference between facets Δ E pzc (ranging from 0.2 to 0.6 V). E pzc values in the literature for Au(111) are around 0.5 V vs. SHE, , , while for Au(110) they are around 0.2 V vs. SHE, indicating that the investigated values are close to the experimental range. (iv) facet ratio x , and (v) varying Helmholtz capacitances C H between the two facets (see Figure ). From these simulations, we reveal four distinct regimes and construct a phase diagram mapping the number of capacitance minima onto the dimensionless parameters L /λ D and e Δ E pzc / k B T . Double Layer Capacitance Profiles According to classical Gouy-Chapman theory, for homogeneous surfaces a minimum in capacitance is found at the PZC. Since multi-faceted surfaces are characterized by several PZCs for the different facets, it is interesting to ask the question whether, in the absence of adsorbates, a multi-faceted surface will lead to the emergence of one or several capacitance minima. In Figure a we show the capacitance curves obtained at large Δ E pzc (Δ E pzc = 0.6 V) for different facet sizes L in a 0.1 mM solution, resulting in λ D = 30 nm. As one can see, the capacitance curves evolve from exhibiting a single minimum to two distinct minima as L increases from 1 to 100 nm. In the single-minimum regime, the minimum lies close to the average PZC of the two facets ( E pzc ave ). In contrast, in the two-minima regime, the minima are located near the PZCs of the individual facets. Together with the results at higher concentration ( c b = 10 mM, λ D = 3 nm) in Figure b, we found that a single minimum appears when L &gt; λ D , while two minima emerge when L &lt; λ D , consistent with previous findings on multifaceted surfaces , , and also a recent study on supported nanoparticle system. In both cases, as L /λ D increases, the explicitly simulated curves converge toward the weighted-average capacitance of the individual facets (compare golden-solid and grey-dashed lines in Figure a,b). To understand this behaviour, we turn to the spatial representations presented in Figure a,b. When L &lt; λ D , the ions reside (on average) so far from the electrode surface that they cannot “sense” the local electric field variations caused by different facets. The surface therefore appears electrostatically homogeneous to the ions, and the capacitance curve shows a single minimum as expected from Gouy-Chapman theory. Under these conditions ( L &lt; λ D ), the weighted-average model thus fails, as it assumes the facets to charge independently. Contrarily, when L &gt; λ D charging can be well described by a weighted average. This is expected, as facet lengths larger than the Debye length will essentially decouple the double layers of the individual facets, as shown in Figure b. Interestingly, the decoupling of the double layers will not always lead to the appearance of two minima in the capacitance curves as one might naively expect. At small Δ E pzc , the capacitance curves seemingly form a Gouy-Chapman minimum at the global PZC even for L ≫ λ D . However, this is not really the case. For L &gt; λ D , we expect the weighted average approach to hold. This is indeed true, as can be seen by comparing the grey and golden lines in Figure c,d. Yet, if Δ E pzc is small, this only leads to a single minimum. To rationalize this, we have to realize that, in the potential region in which the Gouy-Chapman capacitance ( C GC ) dominates, the C GC ( E ) of a single facet in a monovalent electrolyte solution is given by a hyperbolic cosine (cosh) function, C GC ( E ) = ε S λ D cosh ( e 0 ( E − E pzc ) 2 k B T ) 10 In this formula, the location of the minimum of C GC is determined by the E pzc and the broadness of the capacitance curve is determined by λ D . The weighted average approach (which holds at L &gt; λ D ) predicts that the overall capacitance should be the weighted sum the individual capacitances. Adding two cosh functions, however, will always lead to one minimum and not two, independently of the spacing of the minima or the width (see Figure a). This is similar to the adding two parabolic functions, which always results in one parabolic function with one minimum rather than a function with two minima. Therefore, if the difference in the PZC values of the two facets Δ E pzc is so small that the capacitance curves pertaining to each of the facets is dominated by the Gouy-Chapman capacitance, a single minimum in capacitance will appear even if L &gt; λ D (see Figure b). Only if Δ E pzc is large enough to space the individual charging curves to an extent that one minimum overlaps with a region in which the Helmholtz capacitance dominates, will two minima be observed (see Figure c). 4 Schematic representation of the addition of (a) two Gouy-Chapman capacitances (hyperbolic cosine function), (b) two capacitance curves in which Δ E pzc lies within the Gouy-Chapman dominated region, leading to a single capacitance minimum, and (c) two capacitance curves with large Δ E pzc , potentially leading to two observable capacitance minima. To further illustrate the interplay among the capacitance profile, Δ E pzc , and L /λ D , we performed additional simulations across a wide range of Δ E pzc , and L /λ D values and summarized the results in the phase diagram in Figure S1 . When L &lt; λ D , all cases exhibit a single capacitance minimum. In contrast, when L ≥ λ D , either one or two minima can appear depending on Δ E pzc . The single minimum appearing at L ≥ λ D can easily be mistaken for a Gouy-Chapman minimum, in particular as the minimum resides at the global PZC for the symmetric case ( x = 0.5) discussed so far. Assigning this minimum to a Gouy-Chapman minimum is, however, incorrect, as it rather arises from the superposition of the two individual Gouy–Chapman minima of the constituent facets. The further implications of this will be discussed in the in Sections “ Interpretation of Capacitance Minima ” and “ Parsons-Zobel Slopes ”. The discussion above is based on the symmetric case, where the two facets have equal area fractions ( x = 0.5) and identical Helmholtz capacitances ( C H ). However, a single minimum can also arise even when Δ E pzc is large and hence the C GC of the single facets do not overlap, provided that one facet dominates either in area or in C H , as illustrated in Figure d and further demonstrated in Figure . Interpretation of Capacitance Minima The occurrence of one or several minima that may or may not correspond to a real Gouy-Chapman minimum makes the interpretation of the minima in terms of physical quantities difficult. According to classical Gouy-Chapman theory, E dl,min coincides with the E pzc for a homogenous surface, as shown in Figure S3 . In the following, we discuss in how far this equivalence still holds for a polycrystalline surface comprising multiple facets. We start with a case in which only a single capacitance minimum is observed even for the independent diffuse layer regime at L &gt; λ D , as illustrated in Figure a. The corresponding capacitance curves are provided in Figure S4 . To ensure this condition, a small Δ E pzc of 0.2 V was used. Figure a compares the extracted double layer minimum with the global PZC. For this example, the facet ratio was set to x = 0.2, as symmetry dictates a perfect match between and E dl,min and the global E pzc for x = 0.5, making this case less interesting. All other parameters were set to the values given in Table S1 . The results in Figure a reveal that, in a system with two facets, E dl,min does not generally equal to E pzc of either the overall surface or the individual facets, with the difference between E dl,min and the E pzc of the overall surface reaching up to around 50 meV, consistent with previous studies. , For large Debye lengths, up to L /λ D ≤ 3, E dl, min is close to the global E pzc . Specifically, for L /λ D ≤ 3 the difference remains within 10 meV in this example. However, this difference increases rapidly as L /λ D increases since the double layer becomes increasingly well-described by independent diffuse layers. The large deviations observed for L /λ D results from the minimum not being a true Gouy-Chapman minimum but rather two superimposed and indistinguishable Gouy-Chapman minima. A similar deviation between E dl,min and E pzc was also observed by Ibach et al., who attributed it to the nonlinearity of Poisson-Boltzmann equation. It can indeed be easily verified that a quadratic approximation to the Gouy-Chapman capacitance would always lead to E dl,min = E pzc , even if the minimum results from the superposition of two other minima. However, adding the next order terms of the Taylor expansion of the cosh occurring in the expression for the Gouy-Chapman capacitance leads to an offset from the global PZC, unless the facet ratio x = 0.5. When a single minimum is observed, E dl, min can thus significantly differ from E pzc unless L &lt; λ D . 6 Comparison between the potential of capacitance minimum ( E dl,min ) and the potential of zero free charge ( E pzc ) of the overall surface for different regimes defined by facet size ( L ), Debye length (λ D ), and PZC difference (Δ E pzc ). Figure b shows the case when two capacitance minima are observed (in this case for Δ E pzc of 0.6 V.) The facet ratio was set to x = 0.5 for simplicity. When L /λ D ≥ 1, two capacitance minima appear, each located close to the PZC of the individual facets, as seen in Figure a,b, as expected from the independent diffuse double layer model. When L /λ D &lt; 1 this collapses to a single minimum, located for x = 0.5 exactly at the average PZC of the two surface, as expected for a shared double layer model. Yet, the two minima do not occur exactly at the PZCs of the individual facets, and the deviation becomes more pronounced as λ D increases. This behaviour arises because each minimum arises from the overlap of a Gouy–Chapman minimum with a region in which the Helmholtz capacitance dominates, as discussed earlier. Since the Helmholtz contribution decreases as the potential moves away from the facet’s PZC ( Figure c), the relative magnitudes of these two capacitances determine the exact position of the minimum. A larger λ D weakens the GC contribution, shifting the overall capacitance minimum further away from the PZCs of the individual facets, such that considerable deviations from the individual PZCs of &gt;0.07 V are found in this example that slightly depend on L . In summary, when L &lt; λ D , the capacitance curve exhibits a single minimum, and E dl,min closely approximates (&lt;10 meV in the examples above) the global E pzc ( Figure blue lines, Figure a). In contrast, when L ≥ λ D , the system may display either one or two minima depending on Δ E pzc , facet ratio x , and C H of the two facets. If the Δ E pzc is small or the facets differ substantially in area fraction or C H , only one minimum appears ( Figure c,d golden lines, Figures c,d and ); however, the deviation between E dl,min and the global E pzc can become significant (up to ∼45 meV when L /λ D ≥ 30 in this example). Conversely, when Δ E pzc is large and the facets are sufficiently similar in size and C H to make both minima visible, the capacitance curve instead displays two minima positioned near the PZCs of the individual facets ( Figure a,b golden lines, Figure b). However, depending on the Helmholtz capacitance of the facets, and the Debye length, significant deviations &gt;70 meV may occur between the capacitance minima and the PZCs of the individual facets. Parsons-Zobel Slopes The capacitance curves obtained at different bulk electrolyte concentrations can also be analysed using a so-called Parsons-Zobel (PZ) plot, in which the inverse C dl , either measured experimentally or computed from models, is plotted against the inverse C GC given in eq . , According to the GCS model, the double layer capacitance C dl is related to the Gouy-Chapman capacitance C GC as follows: 1 C dl = 1 C H + 1 C GC 11 In this framework, the slope of the PZ plot should be unity and the intercept corresponds to the inverse of C H . Although PZ plots can in principle be constructed at various states of charge, , literature discussions typically focus on the case at E pzc or E dl,min . , , Deviations from a slope of 1 can occur due to roughness, adsorption events or ion attraction. , Here, we study the effect of crystalline heterogeneity and different facet PZCs on the Parsons-Zobel slopes. We restrict our analysis to the single-minimum case and use the capacitance value at this minimum to construct the PZ plot, consistent with common experimental practice. , The corresponding state of charge at this point will be discussed later. Figure shows the influence of L and Δ E pzc on the PZ plots. In all cases studied, the PZ slopes remain nearly constant, with R 2 &gt; 0.99 for a linear fit (though small, but noticeable deviations from the linear behaviour are visible in some cases). The slope is, however not unity in most cases: The results show that both increasing L and Δ E pzc reduce the slope of the PZ plot, while the intercept remains nearly unchanged across the parameter ranges studied. Specifically, the slope decreases from 0.99 to 0.42 as L increases from 1 to 100 nm, and from 1.00 to 0.47 as Δ E pzc increases from 0 V (homogenous surface) to 0.3 V. 7 Effect of (a) facet size L and (b) PZC difference (Δ E pzc ) on Parsons-Zobel (PZ) plots. The base case (orange line) uses L = 30 nm, Δ E pzc = 0.2 V, x = 0.5, and C H 1 = C H 2 = 50 μF/cm 2 . The dashed lines represents linear fits to the data. The missing data point for (b) at low 1/ C GC occurs because two capacitance minima are present in that case. The dependence of the PZ slope on L can be rationalized by considering Figure c: the capacitance minimum increases with L , approaching the weighted average of the single-facet values. This occurs because, even at zero net charge ( E dl, min ), the two facets carry equal and opposite charges (see the potential distribution in Figures and S6 ), resulting in a total capacitance larger than the single-facet value at its PZC. Similarly, increasing Δ E pzc will also increase the charge on each facet at E dl, min , which increases the capacitance and decreases the PZ slope. This result has also been demonstrated by Ibach et al. Influence of Adsorbates The influence of adsorbates on polycrystalline electrodes is of particular importance, as both experimental and computational studies have revealed that low-coordinated facets possess a higher tendency for water dissociation and are therefore often covered by adsorbates, especially for oxophilic metals such as Pt. − To capture these effects, we investigate how adsorbates modify the capacitance curves and corresponding PZ plots of polycrystalline electrodes. Model Implications and Limitations Building on the above analysis, we now discuss the implications of the model for interpreting experimental data. Experimentally, a capacitance minimum is often assumed to coincide with the PZC of the interface. One of the main take-home messages from this study is that this equivalence can break down in systems with multiple surface facets or in the presence of specific adsorption ( Figures and ). The pure fact that the position of the minimum does not shift with concentration is insufficient to exclude multi-facet effects. A practical method to minimizing the difference between the E dl,min and E pzc suggested by this study is to employ highly dilute electrolytes and surfaces with small facet sizes, which correspond to low L /λ D ratio ( Figure ). Another common experimental observation is that the PZ slope is smaller than unity, usually attributed to the presence of specific adsorption or surface roughness. , , Here, we identify an additional mechanism: increasing the facet size (effectively reducing roughness, given that different facets have distinct geometries or double layer properties) can also lower the PZ slope. This effect arises because, at larger L , the two facets behave more like independent electrodes, and the system is described by the weighted-average model well. Yet, if the local PZCs of the surfaces are similar, this will not show up via two different minima. Instead, a single minimum at E dl,min will be found even if L &gt; λ D . At this E dl,min , however, the facets are not uncharged, but carry equal and opposite charges, resulting in a total capacitance larger than that of a single facet at its own PZC, and thereby reducing the PZ slope. The present model provides a systematic framework to quantify the effects of surface heterogeneity and adsorbates on double-layer properties, and enables a direct comparison between E dl,min and E pzc within a unified framework, which remains challenging for both experimental techniques and AIMD simulations. Nonetheless, the limitations and possible extensions of the model should be acknowledged. First, the model currently treats only two facets and neglects explicit treatment of facet boundaries and spatial misalignment of facets, whereas real polycrystalline electrodes consist of many facets with random distributions, separated by grain boundaries. , Future studies could extend the model to treat multiple randomly distributed facets and quantify the role of grain boundaries and surface misalignment in shaping double-layer characteristics. Second, the model neglects atomic-level structural details and instead approximates the surface as distinct but otherwise featureless regions, each characterized by a different E pzc and C H . Similarly, adsorbates are represented implicitly through a Langmuir-type C ads and a reduced C H , without explicitly accounting for their explicit electronic interactions. Coupling this continuum framework with DFT calculations would allow explicit incorporation of atomic-scale structure, metal–adsorbate interactions , and facet-specific electronic interactions. Conclusions In this study, we developed a mean-field model to investigate how surface heterogeneity and adsorbates shape the EDL of polycrystalline electrodes, with a focus on their impact on capacitance, PZC and PZ plots. Our analysis identifies four distinct regimes of double-layer behaviour, governed by the interplay of facet size and ratio, Debye length, PZC and Helmholtz capacitance difference between facets. Depending on these parameters, the capacitance–potential profiles can exhibit one or two minima, with the corresponding E dl,min either coinciding with or deviating from the global E pzc by up to tens of meV. Furthermore, we found that increasing the facet size (effectively reducing roughness) and PZC difference between facets lowers the PZ slope, arising from facet–facet charge compensation at the capacitance minimum. Incorporating adsorption on low-coordinated facets further reveals how specific adsorption modifies both the symmetry and magnitude of capacitance curves and suppresses the PZ slope. Specifically, a single adsorbate at 8% coverage decreases the slope from 1 to ∼0.4, which remains higher than the experimentally measured PZ slope on Pt(111). This discrepancy highlights the likely involvement of multiple adsorbates or additional effects beyond specific adsorption.
